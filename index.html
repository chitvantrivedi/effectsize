Effect size and statistical power

Effect size says how big a difference is. Power says how likely a study is to detect a difference of that size. This page uses the comparison of two groups, such as a treatment group and a control group.

1. Effect size: the difference, measured in units of spread

A 5-point difference is large when scores barely vary and tiny when they vary a lot. Cohen's d divides the difference by the standard deviation, so it means the same thing on any scale. Rough benchmarks: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.

2. Power: will the study notice the effect?

Run the same study again and again. Each run produces a t statistic, which measures how far apart the sample means are relative to their noise. With no real effect, t scatters around 0 (grey curve), and a few runs still cross the cutoff by luck (violet, false positives). With a real effect, t scatters around a higher value (teal curve). Power is the teal share past the cutoff; the orange share is real effects the study missed.

3. Planning: how many people do you need?

Power rises with sample size and with effect size. Small effects need far larger samples: halving the effect size roughly quadruples the people needed. Dashed line = target power.
